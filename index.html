Immunity Basics

Ch 16 — the parts of the immune system, how immunity is acquired, self vs non-self, cells of immunity, and age-related change.

⚡ Quick Hits

  • Two parts: innate (first line, nonspecific, quick, present at birth) + adaptive (second, specific, learned).
  • Full immunity (immunocompetence) needs all 3 processes: inflammation + AMI + CMI.
  • HLAs = person’s “universal product code”; identical only to an identical sibling.
  • Immunity is most efficient in the 20s–30s, then slowly declines.
  • Older adults: may have no fever and no leukocytosis with infection; TB skin tests may be false-negative.

Two Parts of the Immune System

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWInnate immune system = the body’s first line of general defense, present at birth; nonspecific, quick action.
  • Innate = skin, cornea, mucous membranes of respiratory, GI, and genitourinary systems.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWAdaptive immune system = second, specific response when exposed to microbes or chemicals; uses specific antigens to generate a response.
  • Adaptive works two ways: B lymphocytes make antibodies (specific proteins) after exposure; and action/interaction of WBCs (cell-mediated).
  • After first exposure and antibody creation, the body recognizes the invader on re-exposure and protects against it.
ℹ RationaleImmunity = protection from illness or disease maintained by the body’s physiologic defense mechanisms. Inflammation = a nonspecific syndrome of normal tissue responses to cellular injury, allergy, or pathogen invasion.

Self vs Non-Self & Self-Tolerance

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWSelf-tolerance = immune cells’ ability to tell self from non-self and avoid harming self cells.
  • Made possible by different cell-membrane proteins and T-regulator cells (Tregs).
  • Non-self = infected body cells, cancer cells, cells from other people, invading organisms.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWHLAs (human leukocyte antigens) = unique, genetically determined surface proteins = a person’s tissue type / “universal product code.”
  • Also called human histocompatibility antigens and class I antigens; found on most body cells (not just leukocytes); inherited from both parents.
  • If an encountered cell’s HLAs match → “self,” not attacked; if not a perfect match → non-self → neutralize, destroy, or eliminate.
  • One adult’s HLAs are antigens (proteins capable of triggering an immune response) to another adult.

Three Processes for Full Immunity

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWProtection requires (1) inflammation (innate) + (2) antibody-mediated immunity (AMI) + (3) cell-mediated immunity (CMI).
  • Each uses different defensive actions and each influences/needs help from the other two.
  • Overlap of all three = immunocompetence.

Age-Related Changes: Inflammation (Older Adult)

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Neutrophil function reduced (counts may be normal) → ↑ infection risk.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWLeukocytosis does not occur during acute infection → may be infected without expected WBC rise.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWOlder adults may not have a fever → minor infections overlooked until severely infected or septic.
  • Microbiome changes → overgrowth of pathogenic organisms; neutrophils & macrophages reduced in number and function; TLRs decrease.
⚠ Safety AlertBecause usual signs are absent, infection may not be identified until sepsis is present — assess older adults closely.

Microbiome

  • Microbiome = microorganisms of normal flora that coexist in and on the body.
  • Found in: mouth, rest of GI tract, nose and sinuses, vagina, and on skin.
  • Differs somewhat person to person; nonpathogenic when confined to the expected area.
  • Helps promote effectiveness of immunity and cellular regulation.
✅ Clinical PearlOpportunistic infections in HIV stage III come from overgrowth of microbiome organisms (e.g., Candida albicans) normally kept in check — ties Ch 16 to Ch 17.

Immunocompetence, Infection & Immunity Concerns

  • ⭐ MUST KNOWImmunocompetent = all parts of immunity working well; maximum protection against infection.
  • Infection = invasion of pathogens that multiply and cause disease or illness.
  • Immunity can be affected by diseases, injuries, medical therapies — temporarily or permanently reduced → health compromised.
  • Concerns also arise when immunity occurs in excess or at inappropriate times (hypersensitivity, autoimmunity — Ch 17).
  • Adults interact with bacteria, viruses, molds, spores, pollens, protozoa, and cells of other people/animals; most invasions do not cause illness because of proper immunity.

Toll-Like Receptors (TLRs)

  • TLRs on immune cells (innate & adaptive) are key for recognition of non-self, especially microorganisms.
  • Also help recognize injured, stressed, or unhealthy self cells.
  • Present on immune cells of humans, animals, insects, and plants.
  • Number of TLRs decreases with aging → less pathogen recognition.

Organization: Bone Marrow & Stem Cells

  • Immune system is throughout the body, not confined to one organ.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWBone marrow = source of all blood cells and many immune cells; produces pluripotent stem cells.
  • Stem cells are undifferentiated — not yet committed; pathway depends on body needs + growth factors.
  • Example: erythropoietin → stem cell commits to the RBC (erythrocyte) pathway.
  • Central lymphoid organs: bone marrow, thymus. Peripheral: adenoids, tonsils, lymph nodes, spleen, Peyer patches (ileum), lymphatic vessels.
  • Stem cell → myeloid progenitor (neutrophil, basophil, eosinophil, monocyte/macrophage, megakaryocyte/platelets, RBC) or lymphoid progenitor (NK cells, T cells, B cells).

Leukocytes: Granulocytes vs Agranulocytes

  • Granulocytes (large cytoplasmic granules): neutrophils, basophils, eosinophils (+ tissue mast cells).
  • Agranulocytes (fine granules): monocytes, macrophages, lymphocytes.
Defensive actions of leukocytes
  • Recognize self vs non-self to start inflammation/immunity.
  • Destroy invaders, debris, unhealthy/abnormal self cells.
  • Produce antibodies; activate complement → membrane attack complexes (MACs).
  • Maintain self-tolerance; produce cytokines that increase WBC formation and activity.

Age-Related Changes: AMI & CMI (Older Adult)

  • Fewer B lymphocytes; less maturation into antibody-secreting cells → less able to make new antibodies → give flu, pneumococcal, shingles vaccines.
  • Antibody response declines and antibodies last less time → need boosters (esp. tetanus & pertussis).
  • B cells take longer to sensitize; memory cells slower to respond.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWTB skin tests may show false-negative results (decreased T-cell activity).
  • ↑ risk of bacterial & fungal infections — skin, mucous membranes, respiratory and GU tracts.
  • ↑ circulating autoantibodies (loss of self-recognition) → ↑ autoimmune disease risk.
📋 Terminology flagThe Older Adult Health box says “the number of circulating T lymphocytes decreases,” but the narrative text says T-cell function decreases “although lymphocyte numbers do not decrease.” Both statements are in the notes — on a test, the safest answer emphasizes decreased T-cell function.

Essential Concept Points (Ch 16)

  • Immunity can be gained actively or passively, and naturally or artificially.
  • Teach adults that vaccinations need periodic “boosting” for artificial active immunity.
  • Older adults may have reduced immunity yet not show typical infection/inflammation symptoms.
  • Inflammation does not always mean infection is present.
  • Analyze the WBC differential to judge infection risk, bacterial vs viral infection, and allergy.
  • Observe special handling requirements for certain immunosuppressant drugs (hazardous drugs).

Inflammation & Innate Immunity

Innate cells, WBC differential, phagocytosis (7 steps), the 3-stage inflammatory sequence, complement, and cytokines.

⚡ Quick Hits

  • Five cardinal symptoms: warmth, redness/hyperpigmentation, swelling, pain, decreased function.
  • Neutrophils (segs) = 55–70% of WBCs; total WBC 5000–10,000/mm³; life span 12–18 hr; stem cell → mature neutrophil in 12–14 days.
  • Left shift/bandemia = ↑ bands (immature neutrophils) — marrow can’t keep up (e.g., sepsis).
  • Stage I vascular responses subside in 24–72 hr; neutrophil count can rise hugely within 12 hr; CSF cuts WBC production from 14 days to hours.
  • Complement = 20 inactive plasma proteins → membrane attack complex (MAC).

Innate Immunity Defined

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Also called general immunity; first line of protection.
  • Nonspecific, present in health, not adaptive, cannot be transferred from one adult to another.
  • Comprises: inflammation responses, skin, mucosa, antimicrobial chemicals on skin, complement, natural killer (NK) cells.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWInflammation is immediate but short-term and does not give true immunity on re-exposure.
  • Inflammation is nonspecific: a scald burn and middle-ear bacteria trigger the same response; degree depends on intensity, severity, duration of exposure.
  • Example: splinter → local only; 3rd-degree burns over 50% of body → systemic inflammatory response.

Inflammation ≠ Infection

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWInflammation can occur without infection — trigger is tissue injury or noninfectious invasion.
  • Without infection (injury): joint sprains, myocardial infarction, blister formation.
  • Noninfectious invasion: allergic rhinitis, contact dermatitis, other allergic reactions.
  • Infection usually triggers inflammation: otitis media, appendicitis, viral hepatitis.
  • If excessive, inflammation can cause tissue damage; it also helps start AMI & CMI.

Neutrophils

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWMature neutrophils = 55%–70% of total WBC count; complete maturation in bone marrow.
  • Names: mature = segmented (“segs”), polymorphonuclear (“polys,” PMNs); immature = band (“bands” or “stabs”), U-shaped nucleus.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWStem cell → mature neutrophil takes 12–14 days (shortened by GM-CSF, G-CSF).
  • Healthy adult releases >100 billion mature neutrophils daily; life span only 12–18 hours.
  • Destroy invaders (esp. bacteria) by phagocytosis & enzymatic digestion; each cell = one phagocytic episode.
  • Only mature neutrophils can phagocytize → ANC (absolute neutrophil count) measures infection risk: higher = more resistance, lower = more susceptible.

Left Shift (Bandemia)

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Normally most circulating neutrophils are segs; only a small % bands.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWLeft shift = circulating neutrophils shift to mostly bands/immature forms (e.g., sepsis).
  • Means marrow can’t produce enough mature neutrophils to keep pace with continuing infection.
  • Immature cells are of minimal benefit — not capable of phagocytosis.
  • Infection lasting > a few days → marrow releases immature neutrophils → ↓ mature count → limits inflammation and ↑ sepsis risk.

Basophils

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • From myeloid stem cells; only about 1% of WBCs.
  • Release vasoactive amines: heparin, histamine, serotonin, kinins, leukotrienes.
  • Have binding sites for the stem of IgE; allergen + IgE on basophil → membrane opens → amines released.
  • Heparin inhibits clotting. Histamine dilates arterioles + constricts small veins → blood pools in capillaries/arterioles, ↓ venous return.
  • Kinins dilate arterioles + ↑ capillary permeability → plasma leaks into interstitial space (vascular leak syndrome).
  • Stimulate both general inflammation and allergic inflammation.

Eosinophils

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Myeloid line; normally 1%–4% of WBCs; many vasoactive chemicals.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWMost active against parasitic larvae; prolong later inflammation.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOW↑ during allergic responses; very high (eosinophilia) can cause/worsen allergy.
  • Allergic asthma: respiratory eosinophils trigger mediators & attacks.
  • Also ↑ in atopic dermatitis, chronic urticaria, organ transplant rejection.
✅ Clinical PearlSeasonal pollen symptoms (sneezing, rhinorrhea) → expect elevated eosinophils.

Phagocytosis — 7 Steps

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  1. Exposure/invasion — organisms or foreign proteins enter.
  2. Attraction — damaged tissue secretes chemotaxins attracting neutrophils/macrophages.
  3. Adherence — opsonins (dead neutrophils, antibodies, complement MACs) coat target so phagocyte sticks.
  4. Recognition — phagocyte checks HLAs; recognizes target as non-self/debris.
  5. Cellular ingestion — engulfment.
  6. Phagosome formation — granules break, enzymes attack target.
  7. Degradation — enzymes digest target.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWNeutrophils and macrophages are the most efficient phagocytes.
🧠 Mnemonic“Every Attacker Really Can’t Ignore Phagocyte Destruction” = Exposure, Attraction, Adherence, Recognition, Cellular ingestion, (phagosome) Ingestion→Phagosome, Degradation.

Inflammation Sequence — Stage I: Vascular

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Injured tissue, leukocytes & mast cells secrete histamine, serotonin, kinins (bradykinin) → constrict small veins, dilate arterioles.
  • → redness/hyperpigmentation and warmth; ↑ blood flow (hyperemia) delivers nutrients.
  • Capillary leak → plasma into tissues → swelling and pain; edema cushions the area.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWUsually subsides within 24–72 hours.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWMajor cell = macrophage; secretes cytokines incl. CSF → shortens WBC production from 14 days to hours.

Inflammation Sequence — Stage II: Cellular Exudate

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWNeutrophilia (↑ circulating neutrophils); count can increase hugely within 12 hours.
  • Pus = dead WBCs, necrotic tissue, fluid from damaged cells.
  • Active cells: neutrophils, basophils, eosinophils, mast cells (eos & mast cells continue response).
  • Arachidonic acid (AA) cascade: membrane fatty acids → AA → COX converts AA → mediators (histamine, leukotrienes, prostaglandins, serotonin, kinins).
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWMany anti-inflammatory drugs block COX from converting AA.
  • Infection > few days → immature neutrophils released → ↑ sepsis risk.

Cytokines

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Small hormone-like proteins made by many WBCs (and other tissues) that modify inflammation & immunity.
  • Monokines (macrophages, neutrophils, eosinophils, monocytes) vs lymphokines (T cells).
  • Act as messengers; responder cell must have a specific receptor.
  • Types: interleukins (largest group), interferons, CSFs, TNFs, TGFs; at least 40 cytokines.
  • Proinflammatory: TNF-α, IL-1, IL-10, IL-12, IL-17, IL-23, interferons α/β/γ. IL-17 = key for neutrophil-rich inflammation (↑ defensins).
  • Influence AMI/CMI: IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-10, TGF-β, IFN-γ.

Macrophages & Monocytes

  • From committed myeloid stem cells → form the mononuclear-phagocyte system.
  • Monocytes released into blood (limited activity) → move into tissues → mature into macrophages.
  • Some “fixed” in tissues, others mobile; large numbers in liver, spleen, intestinal tract (names vary by location).
  • Functions: phagocytosis, repair, antigen presenting/processing, cytokine secretion.
  • Large, long-lived, can do many phagocytic events; easily distinguish self vs non-self.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWMacrophage = major cell in stage I inflammation (already in place → rapid, but limited by small numbers).

Tissue Mast Cells & Complement

  • Mast cells look/act like basophils & eosinophils but come from a different parent cell, don’t circulate as mature cells.
  • Mature in tissues near blood vessels, lung, skin, mucous membranes; have IgE stem binding sites → allergic reactions; prolong inflammation in response to T-cell products.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWComplement system = 20 types of inactive plasma proteins → activated, join, surround & “fix” to antigen → membrane attack complex (MAC).
  • MAC makes immune cell attachment and phagocytosis more efficient.

Inflammation Sequence — Stage III: Tissue Repair

  • Completed last but begins at time of injury; critical to final function.
  • WBCs induce healthy cells to divide; in non-dividing tissue → new vessel growth + scar tissue.
  • Scar does not function like original tissue → function lost where scar forms.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWExample: MI — if 20% of left ventricle becomes scar, LV contraction is reduced by at least 20%.
  • Inflammation alone cannot give long-lasting immunity — lymphocytes needed (AMI, CMI).
🧠 MnemonicInflammation stages = “V-E-R”: Vascular → Exudate (cellular) → Repair.
🧠 MnemonicCardinal signs “PRISH”: Pain, Redness/hyperpigmentation, Immobility (↓ function), Swelling, Heat (warmth).

Adaptive Immunity: AMI & CMI

Antibody-mediated (humoral) and cell-mediated immunity — the 7 steps of antibody production, antibody classes, acquiring immunity, T-cell subsets, NK cells.

⚡ Quick Hits

  • Each plasma cell makes up to 300 antibody molecules per second.
  • First exposure → IgM (5-member, 10 binding sites); re-exposure → large amounts of IgG.
  • IgM keeps initial illness (e.g., influenza A) to 5–10 days instead of weeks–months.
  • Helper T = CD4+; Tregs have CD25, activated by IL-2 + FoxP3, secrete IL-10; NK = CD16+.
  • Convalescent COVID-19 plasma: give within 72 hours of symptom onset.

Adaptive Immunity Overview

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Specific, long-term resistance; responses are not automatic → also called acquired immunity.
  • Body must learn/acquire the ability after infection/exposure; lymphocytes provide true long-lasting immunity.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWTwo divisions: antibody-mediated immunity (AMI) and cell-mediated immunity (CMI).
  • Activation of both requires interaction with innate immunity.

Antibody-Mediated Immunity (AMI) Basics

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWAMI = humoral immunity: antigen–antibody interactions neutralize, eliminate, or destroy foreign proteins.
  • Antibodies = immunoglobulins made by sensitized B lymphocytes; the antibody (not the B cell) acts on the antigen.
  • B cells have the most direct role; macrophages & T lymphocytes help.
  • B cells start in bone marrow (primary lymphoid tissue) → mature in secondary tissues: spleen, parts of lymph nodes, tonsils, intestinal mucosa.

7 Steps of Antibody Production (AMI)

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  1. Exposure/invasion — antigen in large enough numbers to evade innate defenses.
  2. Antigen recognition — macrophage (after opsonization) presents antigen to helper/inducer T cell; they process it and bring it to the unsensitized B cell.
  3. Sensitization — B cell recognizes antigen as non-self; each B cell can be sensitized only once, to one antigen; it divides into a plasma cell (makes antibody now) and a memory cell (waits for next exposure).
  4. Antibody production & release — plasma cells, up to 300 molecules/sec, antibody specific only to the sensitizing antigen; released into body “humors.”
  5. Antibody–antigen binding — Y-shaped antibody; two Fab arm tips bind antigen; stem = Fc fragment binds Fc receptors on WBCs.
  6. Antibody-binding actions — agglutination, lysis, complement fixation, precipitation, inactivation.
  7. Sustained immunity (memory) — memory B cells → rapid anamnestic response on re-exposure; prevents getting the same disease twice.
✅ Clinical PearlLecture/text example: a college student exposed to influenza A in a dorm gets sick in 2–4 days; their B cells make anti–influenza A antibodies only — preventing influenza A more than once.
ℹ RationaleCirculating antibodies can be transferred to another adult, giving immediate short-term immunity (basis of passive immunity).

Antibody-Binding Actions

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWAgglutination — antibodies link antigens into large & small immune complexes (clumping); ↑ macrophage/neutrophil action.
  • Lysis — holes in invader membrane (bacteria, viruses); usually needs complement.
  • Complement fixation — IgG & IgM activate the 20 complement proteins in a cascade → MACs.
  • Precipitation — like agglutination but larger; complexes fall out of suspension → removed by neutrophils/macrophages.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWInactivation (neutralization) — antibody covers the antigen’s active site; harmless without being destroyed.
🧠 Mnemonic“All Little Cells Play Innocent” = Agglutination, Lysis, Complement fixation, Precipitation, Inactivation.

Acquiring AMI: Active Immunity

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Active = body makes its own antibodies.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWNatural active (e.g., influenza A infection): most effective and longest lasting.
  • Artificial active (vaccine-induced) prevents serious illness (e.g., tetanus, diphtheria, polio); attenuated antigen, oral or injected; lasts many years but needs boosters.
✅ Clinical PearlUnvaccinated patient with deep contaminated puncture wound: first give antitetanus antibodies (immediate, passive) → a few weeks later give tetanus toxoid vaccine (active).

Acquiring AMI: Passive Immunity

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Antibodies made in another human/animal → recognized as non-self and eliminated quickly → immediate, short-term.
  • Natural passive: mother → fetus (placenta) or infant (colostrum, breast milk).
  • Artificial passive used for exposures to rabies, tetanus, poisonous snake bites, other serious infections without active immunity.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWConvalescent serum (COVID-19): most effective for severe or high-risk (e.g., immunocompromised) patients; give within 72 hours of symptom onset.
  • AMI gives long-lasting immunity only when combined with CMI.

Cell-Mediated Immunity (CMI)

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Also cellular immunity; adaptive, long-lasting.
  • Lymphocyte stem cells mature in secondary lymphoid tissues of thymus and pericortical areas of lymph nodes.
  • Regulates AMI and inflammation by producing cytokines.
  • >200 T-cell membrane proteins identified; all mature T cells carry T1, T3, T10, T11.
  • T cells form clones early; healthy adults have millions of clones → excellent at recognizing non-self quickly.

T-Cell Subsets & NK Cells

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWHelper T cells = T4+ / TH / CD4+: enhance all immunity; secrete cytokines that ↑ marrow stem cells & maturation — the “organizers.”
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWRegulator T cells (Tregs): start as T4 cells, sensitized in thymus; CD25 receptors; activated by IL-2 and FoxP3; formerly “suppressor T cells.”
  • Tregs prevent hypersensitivity/overreactions & autoantibodies; secrete IL-10 (inhibitory). Nonfunctional Tregs → systemic inflammation, ↑ autoimmune risk.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWCytotoxic T cells (CTLs, TC): destroy cells with processed antigen HLAs; maintain self-tolerance via CTLA-4; best against self cells infected by viruses or protozoa (“lethal hit” → lysis).
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWNatural killer cells (CD16+): cytotoxic without prior sensitization; “seek and destroy” — best vs cancer cells and virally infected cells.

Protection Provided by CMI

  • Differentiates self from non-self; most easily recognizes cancer cells and self cells infected by intracellular organisms, especially viruses.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWImportant in preventing cancer development and metastasis after carcinogen exposure.
  • CMI contributes the most to transplant rejection (see Transplant tab).

Hypersensitivity, Angioedema & Anaphylaxis

Ch 17 — the four Gell & Coombs types, allergy testing, ACE-inhibitor angioedema, hereditary angioedema drugs, anaphylaxis recognition and emergency care, epinephrine auto-injectors.

⚡ Quick Hits

  • Type I <1 hr · Type II minutes–hours · Type III 4–10 days · Type IV 12–72 hr (resolves 5–7 days).
  • Epinephrine IM = first-line; repeat every 5–15 min, up to 3 doses → then IV epinephrine.
  • O₂ via high-flow nonrebreather at 90%–100%; HOB 45° unless severely hypotensive; recumbent + legs elevated after epi.
  • Anaphylaxis hypotension = SBP drop >30% from baseline or adult SBP <90 mm Hg.
  • EAI: middle outer thigh at 90°, hold 3 sec after click, massage 10 sec; carry 2 devices.
  • ACEI angioedema: greatest risk first 24 hr, but can occur years later; Black patients 3–5× higher incidence; monitor 2–6 hr after drug therapy ends.

Hypersensitivity (Allergy) Basics

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWHypersensitivity = overactive immunity with excessive inflammation in response to an allergen the patient has usually been previously exposed to (aka allergy).
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWAllergen = antigen that triggers overreactions only in susceptible individuals.
  • Responses become harmful when excessive, widespread, or directed at normal tissues.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWPredisposition is genetic, but specific allergies are not inherited (penicillin-allergic mother may have peanut-allergic child).
  • Range: minor (itchy watery eyes, sneezing) → life-threatening (allergic asthma, angioedema, anaphylaxis, bronchoconstriction, circulatory collapse).

Type I Mechanism

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Most common type; target usually appropriate but response excessive; ↑ IgE.
  • First exposure → antigen-specific IgE binds basophils & mast cells → patient sensitized.
  • Re-exposure primary phase: allergen binds 2 adjacent IgE → membrane opens → histamine released → warmth, swelling, pain, mucus, itching, redness lasting about 10 minutes (continuous if allergen persists).
  • Secondary phase: proteins attract WBCs → leukotriene, bradykinin, prostaglandins → more generalized, prolonged inflammation.
  • Local reactions (rhinorrhea, sneezing, itchy eyes) use the same mechanism as widespread anaphylaxis.

Types II, III, IV Details

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Type II: autoantibodies vs self cells with attached foreign protein → immune complex → self cell destroyed (e.g., wrong blood type transfusion).
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWType III: excess antigens → complexes lodge in small vessels of kidneys, skin, joints; most autoimmune disorders are type III (RA, SLE).
  • SLE complexes → vasculitis, glomerulonephritis, arthralgia/arthritis.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWType IV occurs 12–72 hr after exposure; resolves in 5–7 days.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWIn type IV, diphenhydramine (H1 antagonist) does not help — histamine isn’t the main mediator; desensitization doesn’t work — not IgE-mediated.
✅ Clinical PearlA pinpoint rash 48 hours after a hike in the woods = Type IV.

Angioedema: Pathophysiology

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWSevere type I reaction involving blood vessels and all layers of skin, mucous membranes, subcutaneous tissue — a deep-tissue problem.
  • Mediated by IgE-mediated release of inflammatory proteins, especially bradykinin.
  • Most often: lips, face, tongue, larynx, neck; also arms, hands, legs, feet, genitals.
  • Intestinal angioedema: severe abdominal pain, cramping, N/V — hard to tell from other acute abdomen.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWMost common drug causes: ACE inhibitors (ACEIs) and NSAIDs incl. aspirin.

ACE-Inhibitor–Induced Angioedema

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Bradykinin (strong vasodilator) is normally deactivated by ACE → ACEIs let bradykinin accumulate over time → delayed onset.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWGreatest risk within the first 24 hours after first dose, but can occur years into therapy.
  • Patients may not link it to a long-term drug — ask about all drugs, especially BP drugs.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWBlack patients: 3–5× higher incidence (possible gene variant); Black women > Black men.
  • Teach Black patients on ACEIs the signs of angioedema and to call 911 immediately.

Angioedema: Assessment

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWTime is critical — airway first; even lip swelling or slight throat itch can progress; defer history until interventions done.
  • Findings: deep firm swelling of face/lips/tongue/neck; trouble speaking/drinking (lip stiffness); face distorted beyond recognition; nasal swelling blocks breathing.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWNeeds immediate intervention: inability to swallow, lump in the throat, stridor (larynx involved).
  • Diagnosed on S&S — no specific test; don’t waste time on labs.
  • Once stable, ask about past episodes, duration, drugs taken, other allergies.
🧠 Mnemonic“LIPS” = Lips/face/tongue swelling, Inability to swallow, Phonation change/“lump in throat,” Stridor → airway emergency.

Angioedema: Interventions

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Goals: airway adequacy + stop the reaction; prompt tx can reverse before laryngeal edema.
  • O₂ by nasal cannula; most commonly ordered drugs: epinephrine + corticosteroids.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWIntubation indicated by stridor and inability to swallow; if intubation impossible → emergency tracheostomy below the level of the edema; mechanical ventilation usually not needed.
  • Drug therapy may continue several hours after resolution (causative drug often has a longer half-life than treatment drugs).
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWAfter therapy ends: maintain IV access and monitor 2–6 hours; discharge if no recurrence. Requires at least a brief hospitalization.
  • Care coordination: determine cause, teach avoidance, seek emergency care for any sign.

Anaphylaxis: Pathophysiology

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWType I reaction involving all blood vessels and bronchiolar smooth muscle → widespread vasodilation, ↓ cardiac output, bronchoconstriction within seconds to minutes.
  • Most life-threatening type I reaction; rapid, systemic, can be fatal.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWMajor factor in fatal outcomes = delay in giving epinephrine.
  • Acute care causes: drugs, IV contrast “dyes.” Community causes: food, insect stings/bites.
  • Deaths from dysrhythmias, shock, cardiopulmonary arrest.

Anaphylaxis: Diagnostic Criteria (Box 17.3)

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Anaphylaxis is highly likely when one of two criteria is met (World Allergy Organization):
  1. Acute onset (minutes–several hours) with skin/mucosal involvement (hives, pruritus, flushing, swollen lips-tongue-uvula) + at least one: respiratory compromise, reduced BP/end-organ dysfunction (syncope, incontinence), or severe GI symptoms.
  2. Acute onset of hypotension, bronchospasm, or laryngeal involvement (stridor, vocal change, odynophagia) after exposure to a known/highly probable allergen — even without skin involvement.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWHypotension = SBP decrease >30% from baseline or adult SBP <90 mm Hg.

Anaphylaxis: Signs & Symptoms

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Initial symptoms may be subtle: sudden severe abdominal cramping, diarrhea.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWEarly sign: feeling of apprehension or impending doom; anxious appearance.
  • Generalized itching, urticaria → angioedema, redness/pigment change.
  • Respiratory: congestion, rhinorrhea, dyspnea, wheezing, crackles, ↓ breath sounds; laryngeal edema (“lump in throat”), hoarseness, stridor ↑ as airway closes.
  • CV: hypotension, rapid weak irregular pulse (capillary leak + vasodilation) → respiratory failure/hypoxemia can follow quickly.
🧠 MnemonicABCDE: Airway (stridor, lump in throat, hoarseness) · Breathing (wheeze, dyspnea) · Circulation (hypotension, weak rapid pulse) · Doom (apprehension) · Epidermis (hives, itch, angioedema, flushing).

Anaphylaxis: Emergency Care (Box 17.4)

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  1. Notify Rapid Response Team; immediately assess respiratory status, airway, SpO₂.
  2. If IV drug/solution suspected → stop infusion immediately.
  3. Be ready for intubation/tracheotomy.
  4. ⭐ MUST KNOWEpinephrine IM = first intervention; repeat every 5–15 min until response; if 3 IM doses fail → IV epinephrine infusion.
  5. Recumbent position if tolerated; elevate lower extremities.
  6. O₂ via high-flow nonrebreather at 90%–100% — even before ABGs.
  7. Normal saline: change IV tubing, flush catheter, rapid NS (or start IV and rapid NS).
  8. Other drugs: albuterol, H1 (cetirizine, diphenhydramine), H2 (famotidine), glucocorticoids (methylprednisolone), vasopressors, glucagon.
  9. Stay with patient; provide reassurance.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWThere are no absolute contraindications to epinephrine during anaphylaxis.
✅ Clinical PearlFirst action for anaphylaxis = inject epinephrine (before O₂, IV access, or diphenhydramine).

Anaphylaxis: Adjunct Drugs & Ongoing Monitoring

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Epinephrine constricts vessels, improves cardiac contraction, dilates bronchioles.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWH1/H2 antihistamines = adjunct (itching, urticaria) — not first line.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWCorticosteroids are not effective immediately; oral steroids continued at lower doses to prevent late recurrence.
  • Albuterol (neb or inhaler) for bronchospasm; CV support drugs = same as hypovolemic shock.
  • Monitor SpO₂, ABGs, suction secretions, continuous RR/depth & breath sounds (bronchospasm, wheeze, crackles, stridor).
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWElevate HOB 45° unless severe hypotension; severe cases → ICU; discharge when respiratory & CV function normal.

Epinephrine Auto-Injector: Use (Box 17.2)

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  1. Remove safety release per manufacturer.
  2. ⭐ MUST KNOWInject into middle outer thigh at a 90-degree angle — can go through pants (avoid seams/pockets).
  3. Listen for the “click.”
  4. ⭐ MUST KNOWHold in place 3 seconds after the click.
  5. Remove and massage site 10 seconds.
  6. Seek medical attention immediately even if feeling better.

Routes of Allergen Exposure

  • Inhaled: plant pollens, fungal spores, animal dander, house dust, grass, ragweed.
  • Ingested: foods, food additives, drugs.
  • Injected: insect/other venom, drugs, biologics such as contrast dyes.
  • Contact (skin/mucous membranes): latex, pollens, foods, environmental proteins.

Allergy Testing

  • Skin prick (scratch), skin injections, patch tests — outpatient, under very careful supervision.
  • Skin prick: tiny amount pricked into skin → environmental or food allergies.
  • Intradermal: small amount injected just below skin → insect bites and penicillin.
  • Blood tests help confirm but false positives possible; less specific than skin tests.

Hereditary Angioedema (HAE) Drugs

  • Used when ACEI angioedema is not responsive to epinephrine + corticosteroids.
  • C1 esterase inhibitor (human) — prevents HAE attacks.
  • Lanadelumab-flyo — prevents HAE attacks.
  • Ecallantide and icatibant — used independently for acute HAE attacks.

Anaphylaxis: Prevention & Precautions

  • Teach: avoid known allergens, wear medical alert bracelet, tell all health care personnel.
  • Emergency kit (“bee sting kit”): epinephrine, H1 & H2 antihistamines, albuterol.
  • If a known-allergen drug must be given: IV access, intubation equipment + tracheostomy set at bedside; premedicate with diphenhydramine or a corticosteroid.
⚠ Action AlertProminently display allergens in the health record; ask about drug allergies before any drug; document allergen + type of response; communicate to the team. Know cross-reactions: penicillin allergy → likely cephalosporin reaction.

Epinephrine Auto-Injector: Care (Box 17.1)

  • Read manufacturer instructions; practice with the drug-free training device.
  • Keep with you at all times; tell family/friends where it is.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWCarry two drug-filled devices.
  • Protect from light & temperature extremes; keep in manufacturer case; keep safety release on until use.
  • Check expiration date, drug clarity (discolored → replace), safety release security (loose → replace).
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWUse at any symptom of anaphylaxis, before calling 911 — better to use when not needed than not use when needed; then call 911 and go to hospital.
  • IM delivery appears more effective than subcutaneous.

General Autoimmunity

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWAutoimmunity = inappropriate immunity against a person’s own tissues; loss of self-tolerance.
  • Autoimmune disorders are common, chronic, progressive, self-perpetuating, incurable (remissions possible).
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWGlobal incidence nearly 4%; affects 3× more women than men (hormonal changes in puberty/pregnancy suspected).
  • Genetic susceptibility + environmental triggers: infection, injury, drugs, hormones, environmental substances.
  • Susceptible HLA tissue types: DR2, DR3, DRB, DQA, DQB, Cw6 — ask about family autoimmune history.
  • Selective (one tissue): myasthenia gravis (antibodies vs acetylcholine/ACh receptor). Systemic: SLE, RA.

Why Autoimmune Disorders Are Hard to Diagnose (Lecture)

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWNo single definitive test (unlike diabetes, where labs alone confirm DKA).
  • Symptoms vague & overlapping — early lupus can look like arthritis or fibromyalgia.
  • Diagnosis combines multiple specialists’ findings + lab markers over time (months to years).
  • Delayed care (poor access, no insurance, distrust of doctors) → often diagnosed only after organ damage.
📋 Prof. NoteProfessor’s lecture notes: diagnostic markers discussed were ANA (positive = suggestive of autoimmune activity), CRP & ESR (inflammation), CBC (↓ RBCs/WBCs), BUN/creatinine (↑ if kidneys affected).

SLE: Definition & Pathophysiology

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWChronic, progressive autoimmune disorder; inflammatory & immune attacks on multiple self tissues → organ failure and death.
  • Autoantibodies mostly vs proteins in the cell nucleus, esp. connective tissue cells.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWType III hypersensitivity: immune complexes precipitate & deposit → systemic inflammation, tissue destruction.
  • Blood vessels are major targets → vasculitis → ↓ organ perfusion.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWArthritis & joint pain occur in virtually all patients.
  • Other: rash, photosensitivity, vision changes, proteinuria, anemia, vasculitis, hepatobiliary disease.

SLE Activity: Flares & Remission

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Symptoms may be constant, reduced (remission), or an acute ↑ = “flare” / exacerbation.
  • SLE activity = degree of symptom presentation; ⭐ most organ damage occurs during high disease activity (flares accelerate damage).
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWLecture: fever = a red flag the patient may be flaring (professor wrote “flare up” next to fever/fatigue on the Clinical Features slide).

SLE: Etiology, Incidence & Prevalence

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Genetic, immune, endocrine, environmental factors; strong hereditary component.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWTriggers: viral infections, drugs, hormones (estrogen contraceptives, postmenopausal HRT), UV rays/sun.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWAbout 1.5 million adults living with SLE — textbook says United States; lecture slide says North America.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWCanada: 1 in 1000 persons.
  • Primarily women of childbearing age; women of color disproportionately affected (African American, Hispanic/Latina, Asian American, Native American, Alaska Native, Native Hawaiian, Pacific Islander).
📋 Terminology flagSlide 4: “1.5 million adults living with SLE in North America.” Textbook: “in the United States.” Same number, different region wording — recognize either on a test.

SLE: History

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Ask about physical changes over the past 5 years.
  • Ask about pain without injury (constant or intermittent) — main reason patients seek help.
  • Family history of SLE/other autoimmune disease; infection/environmental exposure before new symptoms.
  • Unexplained intermittent fevers with fatigue when family isn’t ill.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWDrug-induced SLE: hydralazine, isoniazid, procainamide — ask about long-term use in the year before symptoms.
⚠ Safety AlertAny woman with chronic fatigue, recurring fevers of unknown origin, and persistent joint/muscle pain → report to provider for consideration of SLE.

SLE: Skin & Systemic Signs

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWFirst observable sign may be skin: dry, scaly, raised malar (“butterfly”) rash after sun exposure.
  • Rash initially nonscarring, ↑ in flare, fades in remission; facial skin may darken; alopecia common; mouth/throat ulcers.
  • Lecture: differentiate butterfly rash from rosacea.
  • Intermittent low-grade fever from chronic inflammation — before diagnosis or with flares.
  • Chronic fatigue (stress, depression, inflammation, insomnia from pain, infections) — debilitating in flares.

SLE: Musculoskeletal Signs

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Arthritis, arthralgias, myositis (muscle inflammation) from inflammation & vasculitis hypoxia.
  • Joints: feet, ankles, knees, wrists, fingers; usually bilateral; synovium inflamed.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWBone & cartilage not eroded → no joint deformities like RA.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWOsteonecrosis (avascular necrosis) after ≥5 years of steroids such as prednisone.
  • Fibromyalgia-like bilateral tender points: neck, shoulders, chest, hips, knees, elbows.
  • Assess red swollen joints, ROM, ADL impact.

SLE: Kidney & Cardiac Changes

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Autoantibodies attack glomerular capillaries & tubule basement membranes → protein leaks.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWPersistent proteinuria = reduced kidney function → in SLE called lupus nephritis.
  • Examine urine for cloudiness, blood, foam (protein). Can progress to CKD.
  • Cardiac: immune complexes in any heart layer → pericarditis, valve thickening.
  • Assess BP, HR, pulse quality, JVD, lower-extremity edema, cap refill, SpO₂.

SLE Priority Problems

🔥 HIGH YIELD

  2. ⭐ MUST KNOWFatigue due to chronic inflammation.

Managing Persistent Pain

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Outcome: pain at a level personally acceptable to the patient.
  • Acetaminophen (alters pain perception, not anti-inflammatory) and NSAIDs (trial to find best) for daily joint/muscle pain.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWFlares → corticosteroids ↑ then tapered; follow taper precisely to prevent adrenal crisis.
  • Some states: SLE qualifies for medical cannabis programs.
  • Rest, avoid stress, low-impact activity, minimize sun (sunscreen, long sleeves), moist heat (warm baths, spa tubs).

Managing Fatigue (Box 17.7)

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Outcome: prevent chronic fatigue with nonpharmacologic self-care; health comes first.
  • List activities: “must do,” someone else can help, not necessary; delegate cooking/cleaning; grocery delivery.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWLow-impact + strength exercise (walking, stretching); split into two 15–30 min sessions daily; rest after activity.
  • Box 17.7: do most important first; plan rest; shop online/one store; prep meals ahead; say “no”; accept help.
  • Plan immunizations with provider — most OK except some live attenuated vaccines; good sleep & diet; stop smoking/nicotine.

SLE Drug Therapy

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Combination of general and selective immunosuppressants; guided by patient response.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWCorticosteroids (prednisone) = cornerstone; short-term SE: acne, Na/fluid retention, HTN, anxiety, insomnia, crying easily; long-term: weight gain, moon face, buffalo hump, GI ulcers/bleeding, fragile skin, ↓ muscle, thin hair, hirsutism, infections, stretch marks.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWAntimalarials (hydroxychloroquine, chloroquine): remission; immunomodulating + anticlotting; ↓ UV absorption → fewer skin lesions; major risk retinopathy → eye exams before and every 6 months.
  • NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen, indomethacin, nabumetone, celecoxib) with food; monitor kidney function; low-dose aspirin vs clots.
  • Immunosuppressives: cyclophosphamide, azathioprine, mycophenolate, voclosporin, methotrexate → report fever >100°F (37.8°C).
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWMonoclonal antibodies belimumab (↓ autoantibodies; IV or subQ) & anifrolumab-fnia (dampens interferon; IV) — anaphylaxis possible; monitor during and up to 2 hours after.
⚠ Drug AlertNever stop long-term corticosteroids abruptly → life-threatening adrenal crisis. If ill and can’t tolerate oral steroid → call rheumatology provider immediately. All immunosuppressants ↓ protective immunity — teach infection avoidance.

Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) Infection

Ch 17 concept exemplar — stages, life cycle, transmission & prevention (PrEP/PEP/TasP), labs, opportunistic infections (Table 17.3), cART drug classes, IRIS, nursing care.

⚡ Quick Hits

  • Stage III (AIDS) = HIV+ with CD4+ <200 cells/mm³ OR an AIDS-defining opportunistic infection — diagnosis is permanent.
  • Normal CD4+ 600–1500/mm³ (healthy adult ~800–1000); normal CD4:CD8 ratio >1.
  • PEP/nPEP start within 72 hr (ideally ≤2 hr), take 28 days; CDC PEP hotline 1-888-448-4911.
  • PrEP ↓ sexual HIV risk ~99%; HIV test every 3 months on PrEP/TasP; SSPs ↓ HIV & hep C ~50%.
  • Test everyone aged 13–64 at least once; 3-week seroconversion window; Ag/Ab combo detects 5–7 days earlier.
  • IRIS arises 1–12 weeks after starting cART; AIDS wasting = loss of ≥10% body weight.

HIV Overview & Stages

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Serious, common chronic infection impairing immunity; global public health concern.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWThree CDC stages: Stage I acute HIV, Stage II chronic HIV, Stage III AIDS.
  • Stage I: short; most develop acute infection reaction within 4 weeks; viral load in blood & genital tract high → sexual transmission possible.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWStage I S&S: fever, sore throat, rash, night sweats, chills, headache, muscle aches (flulike) → then resolve.
  • Stage II: treated as a chronic disorder; most are diagnosed & start drugs here after seroconversion.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWStage III: profound immune reduction → OIs and cancers.
🧠 MnemonicStage I = “FRESH Chills & Myalgias”: Fever, Rash, Evening (night) sweats, Sore throat, Headache, Chills, Muscle aches.
✅ Clinical PearlA client reporting stage I HIV → expect sore throat with myalgias (not candidiasis, lymphocytopenia, or CD4 300).

Stage III (AIDS) Criteria

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWHIV positive and CD4+ T-cell count <200 cells/mm³ or an AIDS-defining opportunistic infection.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWOnce diagnosed, stage III remains even if CD4 rises or infection is treated.
  • Everyone with stage III has HIV; not everyone with HIV has stage III.
  • Poor CD4 function → leukopenia, lymphocytopenia, incomplete/nonfunctional antibodies, abnormal macrophages.
  • OIs = overgrowth of normal microbiome organisms (e.g., Candida albicans).

Opportunistic Infections & Cancers (Box 17.8)

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWMost common OIs: candidiasis, cryptococcosis, cytomegalovirus, Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC), Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP), toxoplasmosis, tuberculosis (TB).
  • Box 17.8 also: invasive cervical cancer, Kaposi sarcoma, chronic interstitial cryptosporidiosis, non-Hodgkin lymphoma (certain types).
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWMost common stage III cancers: Kaposi sarcoma (KS), certain non-Hodgkin lymphomas, invasive cervical cancer.

Pathophysiology: Retrovirus

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Transmitted via sexual contact, sharing needles, perinatal exposure.
  • Seeks CD4 cells via “docking proteins” (CD4 receptor + CCR5 / CXCR4 co-receptors), takes over the cell to make viral copies.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWRetrovirus = uses RNA (not DNA); inserts into human DNA using reverse transcriptase.
  • Subtypes: ⭐ HIV-1 (most common worldwide, most virulent); HIV-2 (mainly Western Africa); both related to primate viruses.
  • Susceptibility partly heritable (study of >6300 patients).

HIV Life Cycle — 7 Stages

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  1. Binding — attaches to CD4 receptors → blocked by CCR5 antagonists, post-attachment inhibitors.
  2. Fusion — envelope fuses with CD4 membrane → fusion inhibitors.
  3. Reverse transcription — HIV RNA → HIV DNA via reverse transcriptase → NRTIs, NNRTIs.
  4. Integration — integrase inserts viral DNA into CD4 DNA → integrase inhibitors.
  5. Replication — CD4 machinery makes long HIV protein chains.
  6. Assembly — proteins + RNA assemble into immature (noninfectious) HIV.
  7. Budding — pushes out; protease cuts chains → mature (infectious) virus → protease inhibitors.
🧠 Mnemonic“Big Fat Rats In Really Awful Buildings” = Binding, Fusion, Reverse transcription, Integration, Replication, Assembly, Budding.

Transmission

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Found in all body fluids; ⭐ highest concentration: blood, semen, vaginal secretions, breast milk.
  • Sexually (genital, anal, oral), parenterally (shared needles, contaminated blood), perinatally (placenta, birth, breastfeeding).
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWNot spread by casual contact, utensils, towels, toilets, mosquitoes/insects.
  • Splash on intact skin/mucous membranes = low risk — still see provider.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWReceptive anal intercourse = highest sexual risk (rectal mucosa tears). Vagina has more mucous membrane area than penile urethra → male→female transmission easier.
  • Abstinence or mutual monogamy with uninfected partner = only absolute sexual prevention.

Safer Sex Practices

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWNew latex or polyurethane condom for each encounter; change when switching intercourse type.
  • Water-based lubricant (with condom) to reduce mucosal friction.
  • Latex barrier (dental dam) for oral-genital/oral-anal contact.
  • Latex gloves for finger/hand contact with vagina or rectum; change between sites.
  • Anal intercourse used as contraception in some cultures still transmits HIV/STIs → condoms always.
  • HIV+ patients use condoms even if partner is also HIV+.

Syringe Cleaning (Box 17.9)

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Wash hands; 3 containers: water, bleach, water (fresh water for last).
  1. Fill syringe with water (container 1).
  2. Tap/shake 30 seconds.
  3. Discard into sink.
  4. Fill with bleach (container 2).
  5. Tap/shake 30 seconds.
  6. Discard into sink.
  7. Fill with fresh water (container 3).
  8. Tap/shake 30 seconds.
  9. Discard into sink.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWPattern: water → bleach → water, each 30 seconds, 9 steps in sequence.

PrEP (Preexposure Prophylaxis)

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWHIV-negative, high-risk person takes ART before sex/injection; ↓ sexual transmission risk about 99%.
  • Cabotegravir (oral or injection); TDF/emtricitabine (all people); TAF/emtricitabine (sexually active men & transgender women).
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWBlack box: don’t use in hepatitis B infection — severe HBV flares when stopped.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWPrEP/TasP do not replace safer sex; HIV testing every 3 months; infection while on PrEP ↑ drug resistance.

PEP / nPEP & TasP

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • nPEP = nonoccupational (unprotected sex, mucous membrane fluid exposure, shared needle, sexual assault — plus STI testing & emergency contraception discussion).
  • PEP = occupational (needlestick): wash area, then call employee health immediately.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWStart within 72 hours (earlier better — even within 2 hours); take for 28 days; pre- & post-testing.
  • Drugs: TDF + emtricitabine + raltegravir or dolutegravir. Not for frequent use.
  • CDC exposure hotline 1-888-448-4911; SESIPs reduce needlesticks (injuries when features not used, esp. disposal).
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWTasP = HIV+ person takes ART to keep viral load undetectable → prevents sexual transmission; doesn’t replace safer sex.

Assessment & Sexual History (Five Ps)

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWCDC: test everyone aged 13–64 at least once; more if higher risk.
  • Monitoring every 2–6 months for stage II/III.
  • Private location; nonjudgmental; gender-neutral “partners”; confidentiality except abuse, harm to others, suicidal/homicidal intent.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWFive Ps: Partners, Practices, Protection (from STIs), Past history (STIs), Prevention of pregnancy.
  • Ask risk factors: unprotected sex, needle sharing, historical transfusion, STIs, TB, hepatitis, hemophilia clotting factors.

Serologic & Virologic Testing

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOW3-week seroconversion window → possible false negative; retest if exposed within past month.
  • Chemiluminescent immunoassay + confirmatory (e.g., Western blot), or ⭐ recommended point-of-service rapid antibody test (<1 hr).
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWHIV Ag/Ab combination assay detects infection 5–7 days earlier; positive → HIV-1 vs HIV-2 differentiation → NAT if negative/indeterminate.
  • Home kits (fingerstick, mailed, results ~1 week; have support present for results call).
  • OMT (gum swab) and urine tests if venipuncture contraindicated/distressing.

CD4, Lymphocytes & Viral Load

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • WBC 5000–10,000/mm³, lymphs 20–40% (1000–4000); stage III: leukopenia <4000/mm³, lymphopenia <1500/mm³.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWNormal CD4+ = 600–1500 cells/mm³; <200/mm³ = high OI risk; CD8 usually normal; CD4:CD8 normal >1 (low in HIV).
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWViral load = HIV RNA copies per mL; guides prognosis, progression, therapy effect; uninfected = “undetected.”
  • Viral load can exceed 30,000 copies/mL → more likely to progress.
  • Baseline: 2 tests 2–4 weeks apart; monitor every 3–4 months; after starting/changing ART every 4–6 weeks.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOW“Undetectable” ≠ virus-free — still practice safer sex.

Other Labs: HLA-B*5701, Trofile, Resistance

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWHLA-B*5701 screen before abacavir — predicts potentially fatal hypersensitivity.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWTrofile assay before maraviroc (CCR5 antagonist) — drug ineffective without CCR5 receptors.
  • Drug resistance testing: HIV genotype and HIV tropism (tropism more expensive/complex).
  • Other: chemistries, UA, CBC, toxoplasmosis titer, LFTs, STI & hepatitis tests, lipids, QuantiFERON/PPD, cervical & anal Pap.

Priority Problems & Infection Prevention

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  1. ⭐ MUST KNOWPotential to transmit infection (lack of education/risky behaviors) — stages I, II, III.
  2. ⭐ MUST KNOWPotential for infection & life-threatening complications from reduced immunity — stages II & III.
  • Early recognition = key; leukopenic patients may lack typical signs → act on even a low-grade fever.
  • Hospitalized: daily CBC w/ diff & ANC; VS q4h; mouth every shift; lungs q8h; urine; skin incl. perirectal.

cART Principles & Adherence

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWDrugs control replication, don’t kill HIV; multiple classes = combination antiretroviral therapy (cART) → ↓ viral load, ↑ CD4, slower progression.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWMissed/skipped doses → drug resistance; resistant virus stored indefinitely and can be transmitted.
  • Newer drugs have longer half-lives but adherence still essential; lifelong, expensive → social work referral.
  • Lenacapavir (late 2022) for heavily treatment-experienced multidrug-resistant HIV-1.
⚠ Drug AlertMissed dose → take it and resume the regular schedule as soon as discovered (no doubling). E.g., forgot meds on vacation → once the new prescription is picked up, take the next dose and continue as usual.

IRIS (Immune Reconstitution Inflammatory Syndrome)

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • After starting cART, rising CD4 cells hyperreact to previously unrecognized OIs.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWFever, night sweats, fatigue, new/worsening disease; within 1–12 weeks of starting cART.
  • Pathogens: TB, cryptococcosis, MAC, P. jirovecii, CMV, hepatitis, herpes simplex.
  • Tx: short-term corticosteroids + treat specific infection; ⭐ cART continues (IRIS usually self-limiting).

Gas Exchange, Pain & Nutrition

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWPCP prophylaxis/treatment: trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole — monitor renal function & potassium/electrolytes.
  • Assess RR, rhythm, depth, breath sounds, VS at least q8h; report cyanosis; raise HOB; pace activities; O₂, bronchodilators, glucocorticoids (moderate–severe PCP).
  • Fatigue: postpone non-essential activities (e.g., several visitors, nutrition teaching, ambulating) but still give drugs & pulmonary hygiene.
  • Pain: peripheral neuropathy (drugs/disease), organ enlargement, tumor invasion; stage III may qualify for medical cannabis.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWAIDS wasting syndrome: unintentional loss of ≥10% body weight + lean mass; high-protein, high-calorie diet (RDN).
  • Thrush/Candida esophagitis → systemic fluconazole; soft toothbrush; dentures in chlorhexidine; soft cool easy-to-swallow foods (pudding, yogurt).

Progression Factors

  • Stage I → III takes months to years; single sexual encounter → slower progression.
  • Personal factors: frequency of re-exposure, other STIs, nutrition status, stress.
  • Drugs suppress replication but don’t kill the virus → some progress to stage III; untreated progress sooner.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWHIV RNA quantification (viral load) = the most accurate test for disease course (better than CD4 count), esp. in stage II.

Incidence, Prevalence & Special Populations

  • End of 2021: 38.4 million people living with HIV (PLWH) worldwide.
  • 2022 U.S.: about 1.2 million with HIV; 13% unaware.
  • Disproportionately affects Black/African American, Hispanic/Latinx, multiple races, gay/bisexual/MSM.
  • Transgender adults, esp. male-to-female (MtF): new infections persist.
  • Older adults: any age; age-related ↓ immunity ↑ infection risk after exposure — assess sexual & drug history.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWLong-term nonprogressors (LTNPs): infected, asymptomatic, normal CD4 without ART (HLA class I, chemokine receptor markers) — can still transmit while viral levels detectable.

Parenteral Transmission & Blood Donation

  • ⭐ MUST KNOWSyringe services programs (SSPs) = priority; ↓ HIV & hepatitis C by about 50%; free sterile supplies, PrEP info, overdose response, naloxone.
  • Positive HIV test ever → cannot donate blood.
  • 3-month deferrals: non-prescribed injections, male–male sex, sex for money/drugs, or partner with these.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWTransfusion risk ≈ 1 in 1.5–2 million units; all North American blood screened; window period → tiny real risk.

Standard Precautions & Health Care Workers

  • ⭐ MUST KNOWBest prevention for HCWs = consistent Standard Precautions for all patients (CDC, Joint Commission).
  • Gloves for contact with mucous membranes or nonintact skin.
  • Infected workers with weeping dermatitis/open lesions → gloves or no direct care.

Physical & Psychosocial Assessment

  • HIV-negative: flulike signs (swollen nodes, sore throat, fever).
  • Stage I/II may be asymptomatic; check drug side effects.
  • Always assess for OIs even if well; report clusters (Table 17.3).
  • Psychosocial: anxiety/fear (I/II), enormous loss (III); baseline neuro/mental status; depression, suicidal ideation → report.
  • Document health care proxy/durable POA (name, phone, email), living will, advance directives.

Prevention of Infection Teaching (Box 17.11)

  • Antimicrobial hand washing (before eating, after pets, handshakes, outings, toilet); avoid crowds, wear N95/KN95.
  • No gardening/houseplants; avoid poor-sanitation travel; take temp daily.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWReport temp >100°F (37.8°C), persistent cough, pus/foul drainage, boils/abscess, mouth ulcers, cloudy/burning urine.
  • Hygiene: never share razors/toothbrushes; bathe daily; brush & floss 2 min; replace toothbrush every 3 months; dentist every 6 months.
  • Food: no undercooked meat, fish, eggs. Pets: no litter boxes (gloves if unavoidable), no turtles/reptiles, no raw meat for pets.

Hospital Care for Reduced Immunity (Box 17.12)

  • Private room, limit personnel & visitors (healthy adults only); room cleaned daily.
  • Dedicated equipment (BP cuff, stethoscope, thermometer) & glove box; no common supplies; single-use utensils.
  • VS incl. temp q4h; inspect mouth, skin, anal area each shift; IV sites q4h; change gauze dressings daily.
  • Culture suspicious areas; cough & deep breathe; strict aseptic technique.

Somatropin, Diarrhea & Skin Integrity

  • ⭐ MUST KNOWSomatropin (recombinant GH) for AIDS wasting: must be HIV+, wasting/cachexia dx, on ART; no active malignancy; daily subQ at bedtime, rotate sites.
  • Diarrhea: antidiarrheals, perineal care (report even a pimple), IV fluids/electrolytes.
  • Kaposi sarcoma: low-grade vascular tumor; stool occult blood (GI) & CXR (lung); responds to ART; intralesional/systemic chemo, radiation, topical alitretinoin; dress weeping lesions.
  • HSV/VZV: keep clean & dry; valacyclovir, famciclovir, acyclovir.
  • Ask about complementary/herbal agents (interactions with cART).

Lyme Disease & Transplant Rejection

Ch 17 — Lyme disease stages & tick prevention; hyperacute, acute, and chronic transplant rejection; antirejection drugs.

⚡ Quick Hits

  • Lyme stage 2 appears 3–12 weeks later if untreated; early treatment → resolution within 20 days.
  • Hyperacute rejection = minutes to hours (cannot be stopped → remove organ); acute = 1 week–3 months (even years); chronic = months–years.
  • Rejection signs: fever >100°F (37.8°C); kidney weight gain 2–4 lb (1–2 kg)/24 hr; heart weight gain ≥2.2 lb (1 kg)/day.
  • Cyclosporine → HTN in 50%; grapefruit juice ↑ level 50%–200%; ATG infuse over 4–6 hr; leflunomide teratogenic up to 2 years.

Lyme Disease: Pathophysiology

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWNot a true autoimmune disorder — antibodies target the organism, not self; chronic inflammation & cytokines intensify tissue injury.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWCause: spirochete Borrelia burgdorferi via bite of infected deer tick (Ixodes) / “black-legged tick.”
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWMost common vector-borne disease in North America; reportable; 6th most common notifiable disease (true incidence likely higher).
  • Most cases: Northeast, upper Midwest, northwestern U.S. (also Europe, Asia).

Lyme Prevention & Early Detection (Box 17.14)

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Avoid heavily wooded/thick underbrush areas, esp. spring & summer; walk in center of trail.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWDEET repellent on skin and clothes; light-colored clothing; long sleeves & pants; tuck shirt in pants and pants in socks/boots; closed shoes, hat.
  • Bathe immediately after; inspect body (ticks ≈ pinhead) esp. arms, legs, scalp; check pets.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWRemove tick with tweezers, pulling upward with steady pressure; no nail polish, petroleum jelly, or heat.
  • Dispose: alcohol, sealed bag, tape, or flush; clean site with alcohol or soap & water.
  • Report rash, fever, or flulike illness within several weeks of a bite.
  • Resources: Lyme Disease Foundation (lyme.org), CDC.

Transplant Rejection Basics

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Host (recipient) immunity attacks tissue/organs from other people.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWMajor cells: host natural killer (NK) cells and cytotoxic/cytolytic T cells.
  • HLAs rarely match perfectly (except identical sibling) → organ seen as non-self.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWCell-mediated immunity contributes the most to rejection (all three components involved).
  • Combination antirejection drug therapy is lifelong; teach adherence; flulike symptoms after transplant → seek care emergently.

Hyperacute Rejection

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWWithin minutes to hours; recipient has preexisting antibodies (incl. blood group antigens) → antigen–antibody complexes in graft vessels.
  • Complement → small clots throughout organ → occlusion → ischemic necrosis → enzymes destroy organ.
  • Historically kidneys; now extremely rare due to HLA matching.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWCannot be stopped; rejected organ is removed as soon as diagnosed. (Listed as a type II hypersensitivity.)

Acute Rejection

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOW1 week to 3 months after transplant (can be years); most recipients have at least one episode.
  • Mechanism 1: antibody-mediated vasculitis → vessel necrosis (not occlusion).
  • Mechanism 2: cellular — cytotoxic T cells & NK cells infiltrate & lyse organ cells.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWKidney: proteinuria >1 g/day or new ↑ serum creatinine → confirm with biopsy; kidney area tenderness + inflammation signs.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWDoes not automatically mean graft loss — drug management may save the organ.

Chronic Rejection

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • Chronic inflammation & scarring; arterial smooth muscle overgrows → occlusion; tissue → fibrotic scar; function ↓ proportional to scarring.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWMonths to years; response to chronic ischemia from vessel injury.
  • Lung: thickened small airways · Liver: bile ducts destroyed · Heart: cardiac allograft vasculopathy (CAV) — a top-3 cause of death after the first year.
🧠 MnemonicRejection timing “Minutes – Months – Many years” = Hyperacute – Acute – Chronic.

Antirejection Drugs (Table 17.6)

🔥 HIGH YIELD
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWCyclosporine: HTN in 50%; liver & kidney testing; no grapefruit juice (level ↑ 50–200%).
  • Tacrolimus: gum hyperplasia, neurotoxicity (headache, tremor, insomnia); kidney testing.
  • Prednisone: skin thinning, osteoporosis → fall/fracture prevention.
  • Azathioprine: neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, neoplasm; N/V, pancreatitis.
  • Cyclophosphamide: take with food; alopecia; hydrate — hemorrhagic cystitis.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWLeflunomide: teratogenic up to 2 years after stopping — continuous contraception.
  • Mycophenolate: diarrhea, vomiting; avoid Mg/Al antacids & cholestyramine.
  • Basiliximab (monoclonal): rare anaphylaxis — crash cart ready.

Antirejection Drugs (cont.) — mTOR & Polyclonal

  • ⭐ MUST KNOWEverolimus: >20% get edema, constipation, HTN, nausea, anemia, UTI, hyperlipidemia; no grapefruit; avoid large high-fat meals.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWSirolimus: ↑ cholesterol/triglycerides; high-fat foods ↑ absorption 35%; renal injury with cyclosporine; no live vaccines; no grapefruit.
  • ⭐ MUST KNOWAntithymocyte globulin (horse/rabbit): premedicate (steroids, antihistamines, antipyretics); room-temp infusion over 4–6 hr minimum; slow if fever/chills; stop if reaction; crash cart; no live vaccines.
⚠ Drug AlertNIOSH hazardous drug handling for cyclosporine, tacrolimus, everolimus, sirolimus, azathioprine, cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate mofetil.
  • All immunosuppress → teach infection risk; discuss drugs before pregnancy.

Transplant Team & End of Life

  • Team: transplant physicians (outpatient tests & meds), coordinators, nurses, social workers & case managers (finances, transportation).
  • IPEC Roles & Responsibilities competency — shared values, responsive communication.
  • Stopping immunosuppression (choice or cost) → not immediate death but pain, fever, weakness, delirium (acute rejection) → discuss palliative care before stopping.
